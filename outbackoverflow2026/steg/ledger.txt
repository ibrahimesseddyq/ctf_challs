Most nights the entries were short: a name, a heading, the weather. 		 				
On calm nights he wrote longer, about gulls and the colour of the water. 				 		
He never wrote about the knocking that came from under the floorboards. 			  		
The supply boat came on Tuesdays, unless the swell was too high. 			    
Its captain never stayed for tea, though he was always offered some.  		 	  
In winter the lamp needed oil twice a night, and the stairs were cold. 		   		
He counted them once: one hundred and twelve, each one worn in the middle.  		  		
His predecessor had left a single instruction pinned beside the door. 			  		
It read, in neat capitals, KEEP THE LIGHT BURNING AND MIND THE GAPS. 	 					
He assumed it meant the gaps in the railing on the gallery.  		 	  
He patched those in his first spring, with timber from a wreck. 		 			 
The knocking did not stop, but it did grow quieter, more patient. 		  	  
By his third year he had learned to knock back, twice, softly. 	 					
Some nights the answer came in long and short taps, like a code. 			 	  
He wrote those down too, in the margin, where nobody would look.  		 	  
The ledger is in a museum now, behind glass, open to a random page. 		   	 
Visitors read the ship names and the weather and move along. 			  		
Nobody reads the margins, and nobody looks at the ends of the lines. 	 					
That is where he kept the things he did not want to say aloud.  		 			
The light was automated in the end, and the keeper went inland. 		   	 
He took a room above a bakery, far from any water. 		   	 
They say he slept well there, for the first time in years.  		 	 	
They also say he left the window open, in case anything knocked. 		    	
The bakery is a laundromat now, and the window is painted shut. 		  		 
The lighthouse still turns, every ten seconds, over an empty sea. 					 	
Ships use satellites now; nobody needs it, but nobody switches it off.
On calm nights, if you stand on the gallery, you can hear a soft knock.
Knock back twice, the locals say, and then go home and do not look back.
Most people laugh. Most people knock anyway, just to be sure.
And somewhere in the margins, the keeper is still writing it all down.
The end of the line is always where the story really is.
